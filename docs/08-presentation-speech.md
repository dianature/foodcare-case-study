# FoodCare: Presentation Speech

To start with, the brief asked for a recipe app for people roughly 20 to 35, and it was broad on purpose. My first move was not to find one person to design for. It was to find a niche, an unowned position in a crowded market, and that is a different search from picking a favorite user.

I ran that search as an ideation pass, with Claude alongside me using a few specific skills built for this kind of mapping. The output was not a list of features. It was a map of directions, and every bubble on it named a specific kind of person and the exact problem they carry: ADHD and low-spoons cooks who stall at deciding what to make, people stuck on repertoire rotation who eat the same twelve dinners and just want help running that set well, people tracking a symptom-to-recipe pattern after a flare, caregivers cooking around someone else's restrictions, people carrying compound restrictions, two clinical diets at once, a dating-app tie-in for someone cooking to impress a new partner, and bachelors who never learned past five ingredients, among others.

Every one of those directions then went through a competitor check, again with Claude doing the research and me deciding what the findings meant. For each idea I asked who is already there, and whether the gap was real or already claimed. Voice cooking and fandom recipes turned out to be features, not niches, something to bolt onto a real product later rather than build a company around. A single clinical diet, kidney disease on its own for instance, is already owned by KidneyPal and KidneyDiet.com. Protein tracking for the gym crowd is the most crowded shelf of all. Seven directions survived that check as genuine gaps: ADHD and low-spoons, repertoire rotation, symptom-to-recipe, compound restrictions, caregiver mode, the dating tie-in, and bachelors.

Seven directions on a slide are still just an argument, so the next question was blunt: who can I actually interview in a day or two. I went through my own contacts and matched a real acquaintance to each direction instead of hunting for a representative sample. P1, diagnosed with ADHD two years ago, for the low-spoons direction. P2, a bachelor in his early thirties, for that bucket, though he tried explaining his own cooking for a few minutes and then had to run, which told me something on its own. P3 and P4, and separately P5 and P6, for repertoire rotation. P7, in her late twenties, who had been treating her own stomach trouble by testing different diets, for symptom-to-recipe. P8, for the dating tie-in, though she does not like cooking and barely had time to talk. Nobody at all for compound restrictions, on paper the cleanest gap of the seven, and I could not find a single acquaintance living it.

Caregiver mode turned out different. It matched two households at once. Household A is a couple in their thirties. One partner lives with a chronic bowel condition and works twelve-hour days, and the other does all the cooking, for her and, since recently, for his father too, who had just been diagnosed with kidney disease. Household B is a woman in her thirties who had spent a stretch of years cooking for her own aging father and her two kids at the same time, deciding what he could safely eat when he could barely tell her how he felt. Two unrelated households, matched to the same direction independently, describing the same load in almost the same words.

That is also where compound restrictions stopped being empty. Nobody was living it as a problem on its own, but the cook in Household A was: one kitchen, two clinical diets, his partner's and his father's, running at once. The direction with no candidate and the direction with two turned out to be the same household.

Alongside those direction-specific conversations, I ran a wider round, three recorded interviews, one chat survey, and ten quick asks, fourteen people in total, to check the premise underneath all seven directions: does anyone actually want a recipe app at all. The answer held across every bucket. Eighty-six percent had never used a dedicated recipe app, none of the fourteen used one currently, and all fourteen already got recipes from Instagram, TikTok, Google, ChatGPT or Claude, or simply from memory. P9 put it plainly: she was not going to dig through a thousand apps. P3, approached through repertoire rotation, said he just types his fridge into ChatGPT and it tells him what to cook, and in the same breath mentioned that he already buys lactose-free food for his wife. P6, approached through the same direction, joked that deciding what to cook every day is the most exhausting part of adult life when both partners work, and separately told me she cooks around her daughter's fish allergy and around vegetarian friends at the same table. Neither of them was describing repertoire rotation. Both of them were describing a caregiver's problem without naming it that way.

That pointed at the caregiver, not at any single patient, as the actual user. Once caregiver mode was the pick, I went back to both households for deeper interviews, then built research personas to hold the findings honestly instead of flattening them into one composite. Two pains stood out because no single-user app touches either of them: a per-person safety check for when two diets conflict at the same table, and a way to hand cooking to a carer while still seeing that the person actually ate.

Everything after that research answers to one line: someone remembers, so you don't have to. In the product, the cook is Eduard, his partner Iryna carries ulcerative colitis, his father, Dad, is on a restricted kidney diet, and Olena is the carer Eduard can hand cooking to. The user is tired and quietly worried, and the stakes are medical, since a wrong plate can put someone in hospital. But the tone still had to stay warm, not clinical. That is the ownable position: calm, human, and safety-first, sitting between busy appetite-bait recipe apps on one side and cold spreadsheet trackers on the other.

One rule came out of that positioning and carried into every screen: safety is shown by a word, an icon, and a shape together, never by color alone. A caregiver reading a screen in bad light, on an old phone, or with color blindness still has to get the same answer as anyone else.

From the flow onward, Claude built alongside me at every stage, the user flow, the low-fi wireframes, and later the hi-fi screens, but I stayed the one steering it. I set the structure before either of us touched a screen, rejected drafts that drifted from the research, and edited every screen by hand before it went into the file. The tool moved fast, but the decisions stayed mine. About twenty-one low-fi wireframes came out of that pass, on a real system of color variables, text styles, and reusable components rather than loose frames, kept green-only and titles-only on purpose, so the review stayed about structure and not about decoration.

Two screens ended up carrying the product. Whose plate answers one question: can this food go to this person right now. Tonight answers a different one: cook once, split safely, one base meal with the exact per-person change and its safety token attached.

The same discipline held for the hi-fi study. I treated the visual direction as its own study instead of inheriting the wireframe skin, and built three directions with Claude on the same Tonight screen, with real stock food photography in each: Editorial Nutrition, light and grotesk-led; Committed Color, a drenched deep-green screen with amber as the single accent; and Calm Bento, soft, humanist, and the quietest of the three. Calm Bento won, because a caregiver who is already tired does not need a loud interface on top of a hard day. I reskinned it with my own color pass afterward: You in khaki, Iryna in olive, Dad in orange, all sitting on a brighter Kiwi accent, so the three people in the household stay distinct at a glance without anyone needing to read a label first.

The built app runs eighteen screens plus a full design system in Figma. A few decisions there trace straight back to research. Today opens with "Dad ate lunch, he's okay" sitting right under the greeting, because Household B's version of this problem was never about recipes, it was about the "did he eat" visibility loop a caregiver carries all day. Tonight keeps the SAFE and MODIFY tokens on per-person rows, because that is the literal shape of P3 and P6's kitchens once you look past repertoire rotation: one dinner, several diets, one cook. Whose plate takes a photo, a barcode scan, a pick from a known meal, or voice, not just search, because P3's real habit is opening the fridge and going from what is there, not typing an ingredient into a box. The wrong-plate guard only fires when the app has a real signal, being in that person's context, or a scanned labeled container, because a guard that pretends to watch your hands would break trust the first time it got something wrong.

The cook screen for Dad's meal spells out plain physical steps, plate his portion first, before any salt goes in, rather than nutrition language, because Olena is the one who might actually be cooking that meal, and she should not need clinical training to hand off safely. And the people in the app stay as colored initials rather than photos, on purpose, so nobody ends up represented by a picture of a sick relative on someone else's phone.

I have not tested any of this with real users yet, and I want to say that plainly rather than gloss over it. Every decision I just walked through is grounded in the ideation map, the competitor check, and the household interviews, but grounded is not the same as validated. Given more time, usability testing is the next step I would take, specifically on the wrong-plate guard, to see whether the context trigger actually reads as trustworthy instead of confusing, and on the tone across the app, to check that it lands as warm rather than clinical for someone who is tired and worried in the moment they open it. I would rather say that gap out loud now than have someone find it for me later.
